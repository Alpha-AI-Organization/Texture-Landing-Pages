Why there is static between stations.

  1. A band of channels.

    In most countries FM broadcasting uses the band from 87.5 to 108 megahertz. A stereo station takes up roughly 200 kilohertz of it, so the band holds a lot of stations side by side if they are spaced out.

  2. Frequency, not strength.

    FM stands for frequency modulation. The sound moves the frequency of the carrier up and down while its strength stays level. Most natural noise changes strength, so an FM receiver can ignore much of it. That is why FM sounds cleaner than AM.

  3. Why the hiss.

    On an empty frequency there is no carrier to follow. The receiver turns up whatever is there, which is background radio noise from the sky, from electronics and from the receiver itself. It becomes a hiss. Many modern radios mute it, and older ones let you hear it.

  4. One signal wins.

    When two stations share a frequency, an FM receiver locks onto the stronger one and largely drops the weaker. Turn the dial slowly past a station and you hear the hiss fall away as the receiver finds it.

  5. Night is different for AM.

    After dark, medium-wave AM signals can bounce off the upper atmosphere and travel hundreds of miles, so far away stations arrive and crowd each other. FM signals at around 100 megahertz mostly pass straight through that layer, so they stay close to line of sight and reach about the same distance day and night.

  6. The odd quiet night.

    On some calm, clear nights, layers of air at different temperatures can carry an FM signal past the horizon. It is the exception, and it is why now and then you catch a station from another town.

Five voices, one microphone.

  • Marlo Quist

    Last Tram Home, 89.3

    Marlo keeps a paper timetable of every tram that no longer runs, and reads a line from it before the first record. He chooses songs by how they sound through a window at speed, and he never talks over an intro.

    Listen for his pauses. He leaves a full breath of silence after each song so the ride has somewhere to settle.

  • Dessa Ibarra

    Kitchen Light, 93.1

    Dessa opens every show by naming what is on her own stove, and asks listeners to do the same. She treats a pan on a low flame as the start of a conversation.

    Listen for the way she answers a message. She reads it plainly, then picks the song that fits the dish, not the person.

  • Teodor Pell

    The Long Quiet, 97.5

    Teodor records the sound of the empty room before every set and plays it first. He believes a room tells you how loud the night is, and that the music should start just below it.

    Listen for the two times he speaks. He keeps both short enough to forget.

  • Wren Adeyemi

    Request Hour, 101.2

    Wren reads every request card aloud, crossed-out lines included, because she thinks the line you almost wrote tells her as much as the one you kept. She plays each song in full.

    Listen for the moment between the card and the first note. She leaves it empty on purpose.

  • Cass Orlov

    First Bus, 105.8

    Cass gives the weather from the studio window, never from a screen. If it is raining she says how it sounds on the glass, and if it is clear she describes the first colour in the sky.

    Listen for her sign-off. It is always the same sentence about the bus, and it is how regulars know the night is over.
